Background

Why twins drift apart and come back

Pairs trading, a form of statistical arbitrage, began on Morgan Stanley's quant desk in the mid-1980s. The idea: two companies exposed to the same economic forces should have share prices that move together. When a gap opens without news to justify it, buying the laggard and shorting the leader bets on the gap closing. The two legs cancel most of the market's direction, so the bet is on the relationship.

What the research found

  • Gatev, Goetzmann & Rouwenhorst (2006), "Pairs Trading: Performance of a Relative-Value Arbitrage Rule", Review of Financial Studies. Matches US stocks by minimum distance between normalized prices over 12 months, then trades 2-sigma divergences for 6 months. Reported positive excess returns from 1962 to 2002. This is the "Distance" strategy in the Arena.
  • Do & Faff (2010), "Does Simple Pairs Trading Still Work?", Financial Analysts Journal. Found that the profits of the same rule declined after the 1990s, partly from competition, though they rose again in turbulent markets.
  • Jacobs & Weber (2015), "On the Determinants of Pairs Trading Profitability", Journal of Financial Markets. Tested the rule across dozens of international stock markets and linked returns to investor attention and news diffusion.
  • Chen, Chen, Chen & Li (2019), "Empirical Investigation of an Equity Pairs Trading Strategy", Management Science. Builds each stock's twins from return correlations and shows that buying stocks that lagged their twins and shorting those that led them earns returns that are not explained by standard short-term reversal or industry momentum.
  • Krauss (2017), "Statistical Arbitrage Pairs Trading Strategies: Review and Outlook", Journal of Economic Surveys. A survey of the distance, cointegration, time-series and stochastic-control approaches used in this lab.

Why it might work

  • Overreaction and liquidity. A large buyer or seller pushes one stock away from its twin. The trader who takes the other side is paid for supplying liquidity.
  • Slow news diffusion. Information about the shared industry reaches one stock first. The laggard catches up.
  • A real economic anchor. Same costs, same customers, same regulation. Cointegration is the statistical name for "this gap cannot widen forever".

Why it fails

  • Costs. Rules that trade daily are destroyed by commissions, spreads and short-borrow fees. The Coke vs Pepsi tab lets you watch this happen.
  • Broken relationships. A merger, a scandal, or a business-model change (Intel vs AMD after 2015) can make a gap permanent. Stop-losses and re-testing are essential.
  • Crowding. In August 2007, many quant funds holding similar pair positions sold at the same time, and spreads blew out for days before snapping back.
  • Look-ahead bias. Choosing pairs that look great over the whole history and then backtesting on that same history overstates profits. The Lab and Arena always select and tune on earlier data only.

Doing this in India vs the US

  • US: retail brokers allow overnight short selling of borrowable stocks. Hard-to-borrow names carry fees, and the alternative uptick rule applies after a 10% intraday fall.
  • India: cash-market short sales by retail traders must be squared off the same day. An overnight pair is normally built with single-stock futures on the F&O list (most names here are on it), or through the stock lending and borrowing (SLB) mechanism. Futures come in fixed lot sizes, so dollar-neutral sizing is approximate.

How this site computes things

  • Prices: daily closes adjusted for splits and dividends from Yahoo Finance. A GitHub Action re-downloads them every weekday.
  • Every signal on day t uses prices up to and including day t, and trades fill at that day's close. Costs are charged per side on traded value. Cash earns nothing and short-borrow fees are ignored.
  • Cointegration: Engle-Granger two-step on log prices with one ADF lag. P-values are interpolated from MacKinnon critical values (−3.90 at 1%, −3.34 at 5%, −3.04 at 10%).
